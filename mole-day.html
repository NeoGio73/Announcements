Happy Mole Day!

Friday, October 23, from 6:02 a.m. to 6:02 p.m.

On Friday, chemists everywhere celebrate the mole, the counting unit that connects atoms you can't see to grams you can weigh. The date and time aren't random: 10/23 at 6:02 spells out Avogadro's number.

What is a mole?

A mole is a counting unit, just like a dozen. A dozen is always 12; a mole is always 6.02214076 × 1023 of something: atoms, molecules, ions, even jelly beans. Since 2019 that value has been exact, because it now defines the SI unit itself.

Why the mole matters

Atoms are far too small to count one at a time, but we can weigh them by the trillions of trillions. The mole is the bridge between those two worlds: one mole of any substance has a mass in grams equal to its formula mass. One mole of water molecules is 18.02 g, a little more than a tablespoon.

Chemical equations are written in moles, too. The coefficients in 2 H2 + O2 → 2 H2O give the ratio of moles that react, and molar masses turn that ratio into grams you can actually measure. It's how pharmacists calculate doses, how engineers plan fuel loads, and how you'll solve every reaction problem this semester.

Just how big is 6.02 × 1023?

  • 2.4 million years

    If all 8 billion people on Earth counted one atom per second, around the clock, it would take them about 2.4 million years to count one mole.

  • 6,400 light-years

    Stack a mole of ordinary sheets of paper and the pile would reach roughly 6,400 light-years out into the galaxy.

  • 18 mL

    Yet molecules are so small that a whole mole of water is only about 18 mL, a little more than a tablespoon.

Mole Day challenge

How many water molecules are in a 500 mL bottle of water? Use 1.00 g/mL for the density of water.

Show the answer

500 mL × 1.00 g/mL = 500 g H2O

500 g × (1 mol ÷ 18.02 g) = 27.7 mol H2O

27.7 mol × (6.022 × 1023 molecules ÷ 1 mol) = 1.67 × 1025 molecules

That's almost 28 moles in a single bottle.

Mole Day has been celebrated since 1991, when Wisconsin chemistry teacher Maurice Oehler founded the National Mole Day Foundation. It falls during National Chemistry Week, and the number it honors is named for Amedeo Avogadro, whose 1811 hypothesis held that equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules.
